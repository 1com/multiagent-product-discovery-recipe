# PantryPal discovery brief

## 1. Recommendation
Test further before building. The discovery data demonstrates genuine consumer frustration with weeknight dinner decisions and food spoilage, but tracking fridge inventory imposes a severe adoption barrier that existing tools fail to solve [P01:t10, P02:t8]. Furthermore, the rigid weekly plan structure fits only a subset of weekly grocery shoppers, while stated willingness to pay remains modest and conditional [P01:t9, P02:t9, P05:t1, S05]. Low-effort inventory prototypes and alternative planning cadences should be tested before committing to product development [UNSUPPORTED].

## 2. Who the users are
The original target customer is defined as households of two or more people who cook at home on most weeknights [idea_brief]. The research roster examined 10 participants, from which 9 are usable across distinct household types and shopping cadences [P01, P02, P03, P04, P05, P07, P08, P09, P10]:
- Busy working families with children (in-target: P01, P03, P04): P01 is a mother of two with both parents working full-time [P01]. P03 is a single parent working part-time on a tight budget [P03]. P04 is a father of three whose partner works shifts [P04]. P06 was also interviewed in this category but was excluded due to extreme interviewer bias [P06:t1, P06:t2, P06:t8].
- Working couples without children (in-target: P02, P09; adjacent/partial-fit: P05, P08): P02 are two working professionals renting a flat [P02]. P09 are a retired couple in their seventies who cook daily [P09]. Adjacent couples include P05, freelance partners working from home with irregular routines [P05], and P08, professionals who travel frequently for work [P08].
- Shared flatmates (adjacent/partial-fit: P10): Four adults sharing a single kitchen with fragmented individual cooking [P10].
- Single-person households (out-of-target contrast: P07): A university student who eats out or prepares quick single meals [P07].

Shopping cadence provides a critical segmentation axis: fixed weekly shoppers (such as P01 who orders online weekly [P01:t5], and P03 and P04 who do a primary Saturday shop plus top-ups [P03:t5, P04:t5]) align naturally with a weekly meal planner. Conversely, daily or ad-hoc shoppers (such as P05 who buys day-to-day [P05:t5] and P09 who visits shops almost daily [P09:t5]) find weekly batch planning incompatible with their behaviour [P05:t1]. Whether the initial target definition should require a weekly shopping rhythm is an open assumption [UNSUPPORTED].

## 3. Problems that are real
Problem validation prioritises observed past behaviour over prompted statements of frustration [P01:t3, P01:t4, P02:t3, P02:t4]:
- Mental load and decision fatigue: In the family segment, 3 of 3 usable participants report exhausting daily planning friction (raw count: 3 households, weighted count: 2.40 of 2.40) [P01:t2, P03:t2, P04:t2]. For example, P01 arrived home and realised at 18:00 that nothing was planned, falling back on fish fingers and frozen peas [P01:t3]. In couples, 2 of 4 usable participants face severe daily indecision (raw count: 2 households, weighted count: 1.40 of 2.80) [P02:t3, P05:t2]. Note that in P03, a major internal contradiction occurs: P03 stated "Planning is my favourite part of the week, honestly. I would never want an app to take that away from me." [P03:t1], yet also claimed "Deciding is exhausting. I would rather cook anything than think about what to cook." [P03:t2]. Time spent deciding is documented nationally at 22 minutes daily, though this figure carries a vintage caveat from 2023 [S04].
- Food waste and forgotten inventory: Food waste is verified by specific past behaviour across 3 of 3 usable families (weighted count: 2.40 of 2.40) and 3 of 4 usable couples (weighted count: 1.90 of 2.80) [P01:t4, P02:t2, P03:t2, P04:t4, P05:t2, P08:t2]. In families, P01 bought spinach that went slimy in the drawer [P01:t4], and P03 lost 30 euros on takeaway when a plan derailed [P03:t4]. In couples, P02 discarded half-cucumbers, wilted herbs, and expired yoghurt [P02:t2]. P08 discarded food after a trip [P08:t2]. Across European households, food waste is benchmarked at 19% of purchased food [S01].
- Household purchasing and pantry coordination failures: Across multi-person households, 2 of 3 families and 2 of 4 couples experience duplicated grocery purchases and uncoordinated pantries (raw count: 4 households, weighted count: 2.80 of 5.20) [P02:t2, P02:t4, P04:t2, P04:t4, P10:t2]. P02 bought duplicate basil that spoiled [P02:t4]. P04 experienced duplicate chicken purchases filling an already full freezer [P04:t4]. Notably, P04 displays an attribution inconsistency, referring to "my wife" [P04:t2, P04:t4] and subsequently "my husband" [P04:t7], suggesting possible multiple respondents. Flatmates in P10 similarly reported buying duplicates that spoil because nobody checks current stock [P10:t2].
- Unvalidated or weak problem: Interpersonal conflict as dinner arguing is weakly supported [idea_brief]. Only 1 of 4 couples documented arguing over dinner [P02:t3]. The core issue is coordination failure rather than relationship conflict [P02:t7, P04:t2, P10:t2].

## 4. What the evidence says
Evidence strength is evaluated across distinct household tiers, separating past behaviour from stated intent:
- Setup and inventory logging friction: Across all usable segments, participants express deep aversion to manual pantry tracking [P01:t10, P02:t8, P05:t8, P07:t8, P08:t8, P10:t8]. P02, P05, P07, P08, and P10 each stated: "Setup has to be fast. If I need to type in my whole pantry I'll quit on day two." [P02:t8, P05:t8, P07:t8, P08:t8, P10:t8]. P01 noted wanting an app that "just works without being a project" [P01:t10].
- Need for plan flexibility: Stated preferences emphasize that rigid meal plans fail [P01:t8, P02:t8, P02:t10, P03:t8, P05:t1, P05:t4, P08:t10]. P01, P02, and P03 shared identical phrasing: "I want to edit the plan easily. I don't want an app dictating to me." [P01:t8, P02:t8, P03:t8]. P05 abandoned a past meal-planning app by Thursday because failing to follow it felt like failure [P05:t1, P05:t4]. Weeknight dinner preparation requires realistic constraints of thirty minutes at most [P01:t1, P01:t8, P04:t8].
- Existing workarounds: Households currently patch together simple disconnected tools, including shared list apps [P02:t6, P04:t6, P10:t6], paper pads on the fridge [P03:t6, P09:t6], notes apps [P01:t6, P02:t9], and recipe screenshots [P01:t6]. P02 stated using a shared list app [P02:t6], but later mentioned keeping a notes app when discussing price [P02:t9]. P09 relies on an ingrained routine developed over forty years, showing no desire for digital planning [P09:t1, P09:t2]. Meal planning apps are currently used by 11% of households [S03].
- Stated willingness to pay: WTP is conditional, unvalidated, and ranges from free ad-supported access up to 6 euros per month [P01:t9, P02:t9, P03:t9, P04:t9, P05:t9, P09:t9, P10:t9]. P01, P04, and P10 phrased their expectation identically: "A few euros a month, maybe, if it clearly saves me money on groceries." [P01:t9, P04:t9, P10:t9]. P02 cited "Maybe five or six euros." conditional on beating a notes app [P02:t9]. P03, P05, and P09 stated they would not pay and prefer a free or ad-supported version [P03:t9, P05:t9, P09:t9]. P08 stated a willingness to pay 30 euros per month [P08:t9], but this represents an extreme outlier anchored entirely on a one-time holiday spoilage event where 200 euros of food spoiled, not routine weekly planning [P08:t2, P08:t9]. Median cooking app willingness to pay in market benchmarks is 3.50 euros per month [S05].
- Excluded interview data: Participant P06 was completely excluded (weight 0.00) because the interviewer asked leading questions that prompted their own answers [P06:t1, P06:t2, P06:t3, P06:t4, P06:t5, P06:t6, P06:t7, P06:t8, P06:t9]. In turn 1, P06 repeated verbatim sentences from P01: "It's chaos between school pickups, homework and work calls." and "Dinner is whatever I can get on the table in thirty minutes." [P01:t1, P06:t1]. P06 provides zero evidence weight for any customer claim [UNSUPPORTED].

## 5. Competitors and market
The idea brief asserts that existing recipe apps do not know what users already own [idea_brief]. Competitor data directly contradicts this claim [C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie, C:ZeroWaste Kitchen]:
- Inventory tracking and recipe generation competitors: FridgeChef provides recipe suggestions from fridge photos (free with ads or 4.99 euros per month, verified 2026-08-12) [C:FridgeChef]. PantryGenie tracks pantry inventory with barcode scanning and expiry alerts at 3.99 euros per month (verified 2026-06-18) [C:PantryGenie]. ZeroWaste Kitchen offers use-it-up leftover recipes for free via donations, though its listing was last verified on 2025-01-20 and may be outdated [C:ZeroWaste Kitchen].
- Weekly meal planning competitors: MealLoop delivers weekly plans with automatic grocery lists at 6.99 euros per month or 49.99 euros per year (verified 2026-07-30) [C:MealLoop]. SupperSync targets shared households with meal planning and chore rotas at 4.49 euros per month per household (verified 2026-08-25) [C:SupperSync]. PlanPlate offers calendar meal planning at 2.99 euros per month, but its listing has not been verified since 2024-11-05 and may be outdated [C:PlanPlate]. WeekWise offers budget plans with store price comparisons for free or a 29.99 euro one-off pack, verified 2026-05-02, but its details remain unsourced as no source URL exists [C:WeekWise].
- Market differentiation: True differentiation cannot rely on inventory tracking alone or weekly meal planning alone, as both exist in isolation [C:FridgeChef, C:MealLoop, C:PantryGenie]. Differentiation requires successfully connecting existing pantry inventory to dynamic weekly dinner planning and automated grocery replenishment without manual logging friction [C:MealLoop, C:PantryGenie].
- Economic context: Two-person households spend an average of 118 euros weekly on groceries [S02], while discarding 19% of their purchases [S01]. Established competitor pricing spans 2.99 to 6.99 euros per month [C:PlanPlate, C:MealLoop], aligning with the consumer willingness-to-pay benchmark of 3.50 euros per month [S05]. Any pricing hypothesis above this range remains completely untested.

## 6. Risks and open assumptions
- Riskiest assumption (Value/Feasibility): Households will keep an active pantry and fridge inventory updated inside an app [P01:t10, P02:t8]. Across 5 interviews, users explicitly warn they will abandon manual entry immediately [P02:t8, P05:t8, P07:t8, P08:t8, P10:t8].
- Product format assumption (Value): A 7-day weekly dinner plan is superior to same-day suggestions [idea_brief]. Evidence shows ad-hoc cooks abandon forward plans [P05:t4] and require same-day flexibility [P01:t8, P02:t8].
- Target customer assumption (Viability): The product assumes all multi-person households cooking weeknights benefit equally [idea_brief]. However, daily shoppers and settled older couples show minimal need for weekly plans [P05:t1, P09:t1], making shopping cadence a vital gating factor [P01:t5, P03:t5].
- Interpersonal friction assumption (Value): Households argue over dinner decisions [idea_brief]. Evidence indicates coordination breakdowns and duplicate shopping rather than interpersonal arguing [P02:t2, P04:t4, P10:t2].
- Willingness-to-pay assumption (Viability): Users will pay recurring subscription fees. Willingness to pay is strictly contingent on provable grocery savings [P01:t9, P04:t9, P10:t9] or falls back to free ad-supported tiers [P03:t9, P05:t9].

## 7. What to test next
1. Low-effort pantry input test: Test whether households will log initial and ongoing pantry items via zero-typing methods (receipt scraping or photo capture) [P02:t8]. Build a concierge prototype where 50 weekly-shopping households submit grocery receipts or shelf photos over two weeks [UNSUPPORTED]. Success metric: at least 60% of participating households submit receipts or photos for two consecutive weeks [UNSUPPORTED].
2. Weekly plan versus same-day suggestions test: Evaluate user adherence between a rigid 7-day plan and a dynamic evening dinner suggestion engine based on on-hand ingredients [P01:t8, P05:t1]. Deploy a two-week digital prototype to 60 busy working parents [UNSUPPORTED]. Success metric: same-day dinner suggestions achieve at least 40% higher recipe completion rates than 7-day fixed plans [UNSUPPORTED].
3. Value proposition and pricing landing page test: Test acquisition messaging (reducing food waste versus automated dinner planning versus stopping duplicate shopping) alongside pricing points of 3.49 euros per month and 4.99 euros per month [C:MealLoop, C:PantryGenie, S05]. Run landing page campaigns targeting 2,000 visitors per variant [UNSUPPORTED]. Success metric: a sign-up conversion rate of at least 5% on landing pages with price display [UNSUPPORTED].
4. Household shopping coordination test: Test whether a shared list synchronised with meal ingredients prevents duplicate purchases [P02:t4, P04:t4]. Provide 30 multi-shopper couples with a lightweight collaborative shopping list for three weeks [UNSUPPORTED]. Success metric: zero self-reported duplicate grocery purchases over the test period across at least 75% of households [UNSUPPORTED].

## 8. Unsupported or weak claims
- Claim that existing recipe apps do not know what you already own: Contradicted by C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie, and C:ZeroWaste Kitchen, which explicitly track inventory or generate recipes from fridge photos [idea_brief, C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie, C:ZeroWaste Kitchen].
- Claim that households argue about what is for dinner: Stated in the idea brief [idea_brief] but mentioned by only 1 interview participant [P02:t3]. Most participants experience logistical coordination friction, not relational arguments [P02:t7, P04:t2, P10:t2].
- Claim that the meal-planning and meal-kit market grows 25% annually: Market snippet S06 lacks a source and year and is entirely unsupported [S06].
- Claim that 73% of families struggle with dinner decisions: Market snippet S07 lacks an identified source and year and is unsupported [S07].
- Claim that 97% of households would pay for automated meal planning: Market snippet S08 originates from an unreliable blog post and contradicts measured app willingness to pay [S05, S08].
- Claim that only 4% of grocery purchases are made online: Market snippet S09 is based on stale 2016 data and cannot represent current purchasing behaviour [S09].
- Claim that users will pay 30 euros per month: Based on a single outlier response from P08 anchored to a severe one-off vacation disaster rather than routine dinner planning [P08:t2, P08:t9].
- Customer claims derived from interview P06: Excluded from evidence due to pervasive leading questions and copied responses [P06:t1, P06:t2, P06:t8].
