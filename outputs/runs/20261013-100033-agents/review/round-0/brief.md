# PantryPal discovery brief

## 1. Recommendation
The recommendation is to test further before committing engineering resources to build the product [idea_brief]. Research shows genuine problems around food waste and grocery shopping coordination, but the core assumption that households will maintain an accurate digital pantry inventory is unproven and resisted whenever manual effort is required [P01:t10, P02:t8, P03:t10]. Furthermore, stated willingness to pay is conditional, rigid weekly planning alienates households without fixed shopping routines, and existing competitors already offer ingredient-based recipe suggestions [P02:t9, P05:t1, C:FridgeChef]. Low-cost tests must validate low-effort inventory capture and compare weekly planning with same-day dinner suggestions before building the app [idea_brief].

## 2. Who the users are
The target customer defined in the idea brief is households of two or more people who cook at home on most weeknights, but discovery shows that demographic fit does not equal problem fit [idea_brief, P09:t1]. The research evaluated five distinct segments across nine usable interviews, while excluding one compromised interview from customer claims [P01, P02, P03, P04, P05, P07, P08, P09, P10].

- Busy Working Families with Children (in-target; sample size: 3 interviews: P01, P03, P04): Parents balancing full-time work or shifts with tight schedules, budget constraints, and children's food preferences [P01, P03, P04]. They shop once a week or order online and require dinners in 30 minutes or less [P01:t5, P01:t8, P04:t5].
- Dual-Income Working Couples without Children (in-target; sample size: 1 interview: P02): Full-time working partners who arrive home late, experience decision fatigue, and suffer from uncoordinated grocery shopping and produce spoilage [P02:t1, P02:t2].
- Retired Couples with Fixed Routines (in-target demographic, but no problem fit; sample size: 1 interview: P09): Older two-person households who cook daily at home with stable habits, fixed meal times, and frequent shopping, experiencing zero dinner planning friction or waste [P09:t1, P09:t4].
- Couples with Irregular Schedules and Work Travel (adjacent segment; sample size: 2 interviews: P05, P08): Two-person households whose fluctuating routines cause rigid weekly plans to fail, leading to guilt and perishable food waste [P05:t1, P05:t4, P08:t1].
- Shared Flatmate Households (adjacent segment; sample size: 1 interview: P10): Four adults sharing a kitchen who cook independently but experience communication breakdowns and duplicate purchases of shared staple goods [P10:t2].
- Solo Student Households (out-of-target contrast; sample size: 1 interview: P07): A single student living alone who cooks at most once a week and has no need for multi-person meal coordination [P07].

## 3. Problems that are real
- Perishable food waste and duplicate grocery purchases: Food waste from forgotten fresh ingredients is a verified, severe problem [P01:t4, P02:t2, S01]. Within the in-target segment, 4 of 5 households (P01, P02, P03, P04; raw count: 4, weighted support: 3.30 of 4.20) report throwing away spoiled produce or buying duplicate groceries [P01:t4, P02:t2, P02:t4, P04:t4]. In adjacent segments, 3 of 3 households (P05, P08, P10; raw count: 3, weighted support: 1.50 of 1.50) report identical waste patterns [P08:t1, P10:t2].
- Shopping coordination friction between household members: Friction occurs when multiple household members shop independently without real-time coordination [P02:t2, P04:t4]. Supported by 2 of 5 in-target households (P02, P04; raw count: 2, weighted support: 1.60 of 4.20) and 1 of 1 adjacent flatmate household (P10; raw count: 1, weighted support: 0.50 of 0.50) [P02:t2, P04:t4, P10:t2].
- Weeknight decision fatigue and mental load: Deciding what to cook every single day causes cognitive exhaustion and fallback to uninspired meals [P01:t2, P02:t1, S04]. Supported by 3 of 5 in-target households (P01, P02, P04; raw count: 3, weighted support: 2.60 of 4.20) and 1 of 2 adjacent irregular couples (P05; raw count: 1, weighted support: 0.50 of 1.00) [P01:t2, P02:t1, P04:t2, P05:t2].

## 4. What the evidence says
Evidence across 9 usable interviews demonstrates distinct patterns between observed behaviour and stated intent, with one interview excluded from customer claims due to research-method flaws [P01, P02, P03, P04, P05, P07, P08, P09, P10].

- Past behaviour confirms food waste and duplicate purchasing: In the Busy Working Families segment (sample size: 3), P01 recounted past behaviour: "Last week I bought a big bag of spinach for a recipe I never made. It went slimy in the drawer and I felt terrible about it." [P01:t4]. In the Dual-Income Working Couples segment (sample size: 1), P02 reported past behaviour: "We bought basil twice in one week because each of us thought we were out. Both went bad." [P02:t4]. In the Busy Working Families segment (sample size: 3), P04 confirmed uncoordinated purchasing behaviour: "My wife bought chicken and I bought chicken, the same afternoon. We ended up with six breasts and a freezer that was already full." [P04:t4]. In the adjacent Shared Flatmate segment (sample size: 1), P10 described past behaviour: "Nobody remembers what we already have, so we buy duplicates and then one of them goes bad." [P10:t2].
- Stated preference rejects manual inventory maintenance: Participants across segments state they will abandon any app requiring tedious data entry [P01:t10, P02:t8, P03:t10]. In Busy Working Families (sample size: 3), P01 stated: "Only that I'd love something that just works without being a project." [P01:t10]. In the same segment, P03 stated: "I think the biggest thing is not making it feel like homework." [P03:t10]. In Dual-Income Working Couples (sample size: 1), P02 stated: "Setup has to be fast. If I need to type in my whole pantry I'll quit on day two. I want to edit the plan easily. I don't want an app dictating to me." [P02:t8]. While the phrasing in P02:t8 was templated across several transcripts, independent quotes from P01:t10 and P03:t10 confirm that high setup friction is rejected [P01:t10, P02:t8, P03:t10].
- Evidence on meal planning burden contains contradictions: In Busy Working Families (sample size: 3), participant P03 exhibits an internal contradiction that lowers confidence in planning burden claims [P03:t1, P03:t2]. In P03:t1, the participant stated: "Planning is my favourite part of the week, honestly. I would never want an app to take that away from me." [P03:t1]. However, in P03:t2, the same participant stated: "Deciding is exhausting. I would rather cook anything than think about what to cook." [P03:t2]. Neither statement can stand alone, demonstrating that planning provides essential financial control for budget-conscious cooks even when daily execution is tiring [P03:t1, P03:t2].
- Demographic qualification contradicts problem presence: In Retired Couples with Fixed Routines (sample size: 1), P09 cooks at home daily for two people but reported: "We cook every day, we've done it for forty years. I know what we like and what is in the cupboard. Our daughter says we should use an app for the shopping list, but we have never needed one. We eat at six sharp and my husband does the dishes. That has not changed in decades." [P09:t1]. P09 confirmed having no food waste: "Not really. Once we bought too much for a family visit, but that was a one-off." [P09:t4]. P09 also reported having no meal planning friction: "Not much, honestly. Sometimes I'd like fresh ideas, but it isn't a problem." [P09:t2].
- Stated willingness to pay is unvalidated and price-sensitive: Stated willingness to pay is unvalidated intent, typically ranging from 0 euros (free, ad-supported) to 3 to 6 euros per month, with strict conditions attached [P01:t9, P02:t9, P03:t9, P04:t9]. In Busy Working Families (sample size: 3), P01 and P04 stated: "A few euros a month, maybe, if it clearly saves me money on groceries." [P01:t9, P04:t9]. In the same segment, P03 stated: "Only if it clearly reduced food waste. A free version with ads would also be fine." [P03:t9]. In Dual-Income Working Couples (sample size: 1), P02 stated: "Maybe five or six euros. More than that and I'd just keep my notes app." [P02:t9]. Free versions were requested across multiple segments [P03:t9, P05:t9, P07:t9, P09:t9]. A single stated claim of 30 euros per month from adjacent participant P08 was anchored to an isolated holiday spoilage incident and is excluded as an extreme outlier [P08:t9].

## 5. Competitors and market
Market statistics corroborate that food waste and dinner planning are widespread challenges, but baseline software adoption is modest [S01, S02, S03, S04]. European households discard 19% of purchased food [S01]. Average weekly grocery expenditure for a two-person household is EUR 118 [S02]. Adults spend 22 minutes daily deciding what to cook, although this time-use finding dates from 2023 [S04]. However, only 11% of households currently use a meal-planning app [S03]. Median stated willingness to pay for cooking apps is EUR 3.50 per month [S05].

Competitor benchmarking shows that the idea brief's claim that existing recipe apps do not know what you already own is contradicted by direct market evidence [idea_brief, C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie]:
- FridgeChef (verified 2026-08-12): Suggests recipes directly from fridge photos, offering free with ads or EUR 4.99 per month for premium [C:FridgeChef].
- MealLoop (verified 2026-07-30): Offers automated weekly dinner planning, shopping lists, and family sharing for EUR 6.99 per month or EUR 49.99 per year, but lacks inventory tracking [C:MealLoop].
- PlanPlate (verified 2024-11-05; possibly outdated): Provides calendar meal planning and shared household plans for EUR 2.99 per month, but lacks inventory tracking [C:PlanPlate].
- PantryGenie (verified 2026-06-18): Tracks pantry inventory via barcode scanning and provides expiry alerts with a shopping list for EUR 3.99 per month, but lacks full weekly dinner planning [C:PantryGenie].
- SupperSync (verified 2026-08-25): Coordinates shared households with weekly plans, chore assignment, and shopping lists for EUR 4.49 per month per household, but does not track inventory [C:SupperSync].
- ZeroWaste Kitchen (verified 2025-01-20; possibly outdated): Free donation-supported tool for ad-hoc leftover recipes, lacking structured planning or inventory tracking [C:ZeroWaste Kitchen].
- WeekWise (verified 2026-05-02; row details unsourced): Offers budget weekly planning for free with a EUR 29.99 one-off recipe pack, but lacks inventory awareness [C:WeekWise].

True market differentiation is not inventory tracking alone, but the combination of on-hand inventory tracking with automated multi-day meal planning and shared shopping lists [C:FridgeChef, C:MealLoop, C:PantryGenie]. Competitor pricing sets an active monthly band between EUR 2.99 and EUR 6.99, aligning with the median willingness to pay of EUR 3.50 per month, leaving any price above EUR 6.99 entirely untested [C:PlanPlate, C:MealLoop, S05].

## 6. Risks and open assumptions
- A1 (Usability risk, Riskiest assumption): Households will reliably input and maintain an up-to-date fridge and pantry inventory [idea_brief]. Target participants warn that setup must not feel like homework or a project, stating they will quit if they must type in a pantry [P01:t10, P02:t8, P03:t10].
- A2 (Desirability risk): Target households want and will follow a structured weekly dinner plan [idea_brief]. While weekly shoppers follow plans, households with irregular schedules abandon weekly planning apps and feel guilt when plans fall apart [P01:t5, P04:t5, P05:t1, P05:t4].
- A3 (Viability risk): Target households will pay a recurring subscription fee of EUR 3 to EUR 6 per month [idea_brief]. Stated intent is unvalidated, highly conditional on proven grocery savings, and anchored against free notes apps [P01:t9, P02:t9, P03:t9, S05].
- A4 (Desirability risk): All multi-person households who cook at home on weeknights experience dinner planning friction and food waste [idea_brief]. Demographics do not equal need, as households with decades of routine experience zero waste and reject apps [P09:t1, P09:t2, P09:t4].
- A5 (Desirability risk): Stopping interpersonal dinner arguments is a primary customer problem [idea_brief]. Arguing about dinner was reported by only 1 in-target household, whereas shopping coordination and duplicate buying represent the genuine operational friction [P02:t3, P04:t4, P10:t2].
- A6 (Viability risk): Existing recipe apps do not know what users already own [idea_brief]. This claim is contradicted by competitors that recognize fridge photos or track pantry barcodes [C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie].
- A7 (Feasibility risk): Inventory input can be captured with low friction and sufficient accuracy via mobile automation without burdening users [idea_brief]. Feasibility of automated recognition for perishables without manual correction is unproven [P01:t10, P02:t8].

## 7. What to test next
Before building the planner, the team must run three prioritized experiments to test the riskiest assumptions [idea_brief]:

1. Experiment 1: Low-effort inventory input concierge test (tests assumptions A1 and A7) [idea_brief].
   - Method: Recruit 20 target households (proposed: 12 Busy Working Families, 8 Dual-Income Working Couples) [UNSUPPORTED]. Run a 7-day concierge test using lightweight messaging bots where half the households submit daily shelf photos and half scan barcodes or receipts, with the team logging inventory [UNSUPPORTED].
   - Success metric: At least 60% of participating households (12 of 20) sustain inventory updates through Day 7 and report daily effort under 2 minutes [UNSUPPORTED]. If fewer than 40% (8 of 20) sustain updates after Day 3 due to friction, abandon full pantry tracking and pivot to lightweight staples tracking [UNSUPPORTED].

2. Experiment 2: Weekly plan vs. same-day recommendations test (tests assumption A2) [idea_brief].
   - Method: Recruit 30 households (proposed: 15 weekly shopping families and 15 flexible or daily shopping couples) [UNSUPPORTED]. Run a 10-day test comparing a fixed 5-day weekly plan generated on Sunday against a daily 5:00 PM prompt suggesting 3 dinner recipes from on-hand items [UNSUPPORTED].
   - Success metric: Weekly shoppers achieve at least 70% adherence (11 of 15) to planned meals, while flexible shoppers achieve at least 70% completion (11 of 15) with daily prompts and report lower guilt [UNSUPPORTED]. If weekly plan adherence among flexible shoppers falls below 30% while daily suggestions achieve over 60%, pivot architecture to same-day meal suggestions [UNSUPPORTED].

3. Experiment 3: Value proposition and subscription pre-order test (tests assumptions A3, A5, and A6) [idea_brief].
   - Method: Run a landing page test across 600 ad clicks targeted at multi-person cooking households at a pre-order price of EUR 4.49 per month (benchmarked against SupperSync at EUR 4.49/month and FridgeChef at EUR 4.99/month) [C:FridgeChef, C:SupperSync]. Test messaging around shopping coordination and waste reduction against messaging around stopping dinner arguments [idea_brief].
   - Success metric: Shopping coordination messaging achieves a paid pre-order click-through rate of at least 5%, outperforming argument messaging by at least 25% [UNSUPPORTED]. If paid pre-order conversion across variants is below 2%, re-evaluate subscription viability and test freemium or EUR 2.99-3.50 pricing [C:PlanPlate, S05, UNSUPPORTED].

Open questions to investigate during testing [idea_brief]:
- What are the geographic locations, household incomes, and recruitment backgrounds of target interviewees [UNSUPPORTED]?
- What is the objectively measured food waste and grocery spend over time, rather than self-reported estimates [UNSUPPORTED]?
- Which input modality (shelf photos, barcode scanning, or receipt parsing) yields the highest accuracy with lowest friction [UNSUPPORTED]?
- Do households sustain dinner planning habits beyond week one, or does retention drop sharply [UNSUPPORTED]?
- What are active retention and churn benchmarks for competitors like MealLoop and FridgeChef [C:FridgeChef, C:MealLoop]?
- Who holds primary purchasing and planning authority versus cooking execution in multi-person households [UNSUPPORTED]?
- Can computer vision accurately identify opened containers and items hidden in crisper drawers [UNSUPPORTED]?

## 8. Unsupported or weak claims
- The idea brief claim that existing recipe apps do not know what you already own is contradicted by FridgeChef and PantryGenie [idea_brief, C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie].
- The idea brief claim that dinner planning causes couples to argue is weakly supported, reported by only 1 of 5 in-target households (P02), while the broader friction is uncoordinated grocery shopping [idea_brief, P02:t3, P04:t4].
- Willingness to pay a subscription fee of 30 euros per month is an unvalidated outlier from an irregular couple based on an exceptional holiday spoilage incident and cannot support typical pricing [P08:t9].
- Stated willingness to pay of EUR 3 to EUR 6 per month is unvalidated stated intent anchored against free tools rather than verified purchasing behaviour [P01:t9, P02:t9, P03:t9, P04:t9].
- The claim that meal-planning software is growing at 25% annually is unsupported because it conflates planning software with meal-kit delivery services and lacks a source [S06, UNSUPPORTED].
- The claim that 73% of households abandon meal-planning apps within two weeks is an unattributed market snippet lacking publication or date [S07, UNSUPPORTED].
- The claim that 97% of home cooks want automated inventory tracking is derived from an unreliable blog post and contradicts measured adoption rates [S03, S08, UNSUPPORTED].
- The claim that 4% of households maintain an inventory spreadsheet is based on stale 2016 retail data [S09, UNSUPPORTED].
- Interview P06 was entirely excluded due to severe research-method flaws, including leading questions and duplicate text, providing zero evidentiary support [P06, UNSUPPORTED].
