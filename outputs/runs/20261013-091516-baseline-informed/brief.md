# PantryPal discovery brief

## 1. Recommendation
We recommend to test further before committing engineering resources to build PantryPal [idea_brief]. Research confirms that ingredient spoilage and the daily burden of deciding what to cook are genuine frictions for multi-person households [P01:t2, P02:t2, P04:t2]. However, the foundational assumption that households will manually log and maintain an accurate pantry inventory is unvalidated and faces severe user resistance [P01:t10, P02:t8]. Furthermore, whether households actually want a rigid weekly dinner plan rather than flexible, same-day meal suggestions remains an open question that must be de-risked first [P05:t1, P08:t1].

## 2. Who the users are
The idea brief defines the target customer as households of two or more people who cook at home on most weeknights [idea_brief]. However, defining the target strictly by household size conflates target membership with problem intensity [idea_brief]. Across 10 interviews (9 usable, 1 excluded), user needs diverge significantly across segments and shopping rhythms [P01, P02, P03, P04, P05, P06, P07, P08, P09, P10]:
- Working families with children (in-target: 2 usable interviews, P01 [weight 1.00] and P04 [weight 0.70]; 1 single parent, P03 [weight 0.70]; 1 excluded, P06 [weight 0.00]): Experience time poverty, rigid evening deadlines, and conflicting family preferences [P01:t1, P04:t1]. In this segment (2 of 2 target family households, sample size: 2), users typically do a weekly shop with top-up runs and need fast, simple meals [P01:t5, P04:t5]. Note that P04 has an attribution inconsistency where the speaker references "my wife" and "my husband" [P04:t2, P04:t7]. P03 represents a single-parent family (sample size: 1) where tight budgeting drives strict planning [P03:t1, P03:t3].
- Busy dual-income couples without children (in-target: 1 interview, P02 [weight 0.90]; adjacent/partial fit: 2 interviews, P05 [weight 0.50] and P08 [weight 0.50]; retired couple in-target: 1 interview, P09 [weight 0.90]): Working couples experience late finishes and lack coordination, while freelancers (P05) and frequent business travelers (P08) have irregular schedules that clash with rigid forward planning [P02:t1, P05:t1, P08:t3]. In contrast, retired couples (P09, 1 of 2 in-target couples, sample size: 2) have established decades-long daily routines and experience no planning friction [P09:t1, P09:t2].
- Shared flatmates (adjacent/partial fit: 1 interview, P10 [weight 0.50]): Flatmates share kitchen spaces and staple supplies but cook independently, experiencing grocery coordination breakdowns rather than collective dinner planning needs [P10:t1, P10:t2].
- Single-person households (out-of-target: 1 interview, P07 [weight 0.25]): Single students cook infrequently, rely on takeout, and lack sufficient cooking frequency to experience dinner planning problems [P07:t1, P07:t2].
- User segmentation by shopping rhythm: Shopping habits segment users into batch planners who shop once a week (supported by 2 of 4 in-target households: P01, P03, P04) and frequent buyers who shop daily or buy as they go (supported by 1 of 4 in-target households: P09; and adjacent households: P05) [P01:t5, P03:t5, P04:t5, P05:t5, P09:t5].

## 3. Problems that are real
Problem statements derived from observable past behaviour and weighted interview evidence:
- Food spoilage and forgotten groceries in the fridge: Strong behavioural evidence. In the in-target segment, 2 of 4 households explicitly recount past waste behaviour (raw count: 2 of 4 in-target; weighted count: 1.90 / 3.50; across all usable interviews: 5 of 9 raw, 3.60 / 5.25 weighted) [P01:t4, P02:t2, P02:t4, P05:t2, P08:t2, P10:t2]. For example, P01 bought spinach that "went slimy in the drawer" [P01:t4], P02 reported that "We bought basil twice in one week because each of us thought we were out. Both went bad." [P02:t4], and P08 experienced an extreme case where "we threw out maybe two hundred euros of food" after a holiday [P08:t2]. On a macro level, European households discard 19% of the food they buy [S01].
- Daily mental load and decision fatigue around dinner: Real, though heavily prompted during interviews. In the in-target segment, 3 of 4 households cite dinner decision friction (raw count: 3 of 4 in-target; weighted count: 2.60 / 3.50; across all usable interviews: 4 of 9 raw, 3.10 / 5.25 weighted) [P01:t2, P02:t3, P04:t2, P05:t2]. Users describe that "Every single day somebody has to decide what's for dinner, and that somebody is me." [P01:t2], and report that "The worst part is the daily question of what to cook. By Wednesday I have no ideas left." [P04:t2]. However, P03 exhibits a major internal contradiction: stating "Planning is my favourite part of the week, honestly." while also claiming "Deciding is exhausting. I would rather cook anything than think about what to cook." [P03:t1, P03:t2]. Adults spend an average of 22 minutes per day deciding what to cook [S04].
- Uncoordinated shopping and duplicate purchases in multi-person homes: Grounded in observed behaviour. In the in-target segment, 2 of 4 households demonstrate duplicate buying due to poor list coordination (raw count: 2 of 4 in-target; weighted count: 1.60 / 3.50; across all usable interviews: 3 of 9 raw, 2.10 / 5.25 weighted) [P02:t2, P04:t4, P10:t2]. For example, P04 noted: "My wife bought chicken and I bought chicken, the same afternoon. We ended up with six breasts and a freezer that was already full." [P04:t4]. Flatmates similarly report buying duplicates when shared items are not tracked [P10:t2].

## 4. What the evidence says
Evidence across interviews, behaviour, stated preferences, and methodology:
- Past behaviour versus stated preferences: Behavioural evidence proves households use basic notes apps, paper pads, or shared list apps, and frequently abandon recipes or plans [P01:t6, P02:t6, P03:t6, P04:t6, P05:t4]. In contrast, user statements about desired features ("Thirty minutes at most.", "I want to edit the plan easily.", "I don't want an app dictating to me.") represent stated intent rather than proven behaviour [P01:t8, P02:t8, P03:t8, P05:t8]. Several interview phrases were repeated verbatim across participants ("Setup has to be fast. If I need to type in my whole pantry I'll quit on day two." in P02:t8, P05:t8, P07:t8, P08:t8, P10:t8; and "Both of us, but not in a coordinated way." in P02:t7, P05:t7, P08:t7, P09:t7), indicating templated phrasing where theme frequency must be treated as an upper bound rather than completely independent replication [P02:t7, P02:t8, P05:t7, P05:t8, P07:t8, P08:t7, P08:t8, P09:t7, P10:t8].
- Stated willingness to pay (WTP): WTP is unvalidated stated intent and highly conditional. In-target participants stated they would pay "A few euros a month, maybe, if it clearly saves me money on groceries." (P01:t9, P04:t9) or "Maybe five or six euros. More than that and I'd just keep my notes app." (P02:t9) [P01:t9, P02:t9, P04:t9]. P03 and P05 would only accept a free version or free with ads [P03:t9, P05:t9]. One adjacent participant (P08) claimed they would pay "thirty euros a month, easy" [P08:t9]; however, this is an extreme outlier anchored to a one-time holiday spoilage event of two hundred euros, not ongoing dinner planning, and must be excluded from typical pricing estimates [P08:t2, P08:t9].
- Research method flaws: P06 has been assigned zero evidence weight and excluded from all customer claims due to severely leading interviewer questions (e.g., "Wouldn't it be amazing if an app just planned all your meals automatically?") and verbatim echoing of P01's answers [P01:t1, P06:t1, P06:t8]. Furthermore, P04 exhibits internal attribution confusion between "my wife" and "my husband" [P04:t2, P04:t7]. P02 exhibits minor inconsistency, using a shared list app while comparing price against "my notes app" [P02:t6, P02:t9]. P09 shows internal inconsistency between shopping "at the same stall every week" and going "to the shop almost every day and buy as I go" [P09:t3, P09:t5].

## 5. Competitors and market
Competitor landscape and market data benchmarks:
- Competitor landscape:
  - C:MealLoop (last verified: 2026-07-30): Offers weekly plans, grocery lists, diet filters, and family sharing targeting families at EUR 6.99/month or EUR 49.99/year [C:MealLoop].
  - C:FridgeChef (last verified: 2026-08-12): Suggests recipes from fridge photos targeting individuals at Free with ads or EUR 4.99/month [C:FridgeChef].
  - C:PantryGenie (last verified: 2026-06-18): Tracks pantry inventory with barcode scanning and expiry alerts at EUR 3.99/month [C:PantryGenie].
  - C:SupperSync (last verified: 2026-08-25): Coordinates shared household dinner planning, who-cooks rotas, and shared lists at EUR 4.49/month per household [C:SupperSync].
  - C:PlanPlate (last verified: 2024-11-05): Meal planning calendar with recipe import at EUR 2.99/month, though data is possibly outdated due to verification age [C:PlanPlate].
  - C:ZeroWaste Kitchen (last verified: 2025-01-20): Offers leftover recipes and tips, free/donation-supported, though data is possibly outdated due to verification age [C:ZeroWaste Kitchen].
  - C:WeekWise (last verified: 2026-05-02): Budget meal planner priced at Free with a EUR 29.99 one-off pack; however, its source URL is missing and details are unsourced [C:WeekWise].
- Market differentiation: The idea brief claims existing apps give ideas but do not know what you already own [idea_brief]. This claim is contradicted by competitor data showing existing inventory trackers (C:PantryGenie) and photo-based ingredient apps (C:FridgeChef) [C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie, idea_brief]. True differentiation lies only in combining inventory awareness with weekly planning and coordinated grocery shopping, not in either capability alone [C:FridgeChef, C:MealLoop, C:PantryGenie].
- Market sizing and pricing benchmarks: European two-person households spend an average of EUR 118 per week on groceries [S02]. Only 11% of households currently use a meal-planning app [S03]. Median stated willingness to pay for cooking apps is EUR 3.50 per month [S05]. Competitor subscription prices range from EUR 2.99 to EUR 6.99 per month [C:MealLoop, C:PlanPlate]. Viable pricing hypotheses must remain within the EUR 2.99 to EUR 4.99 monthly band [C:PantryGenie, C:SupperSync, S05].

## 6. Risks and open assumptions
Key risks and unvalidated assumptions:
- Pantry inventory maintenance friction (Highest Risk): PantryPal assumes households will keep an active pantry inventory up to date [idea_brief]. Multiple participants warn that high-effort setup will cause immediate churn ("If I need to type in my whole pantry I'll quit on day two." - P02:t8, P05:t8, P10:t8; "I'd love something that just works without being a project." - P01:t10, P10:t10) [P01:t10, P02:t8, P05:t8, P10:t8]. If logging ingredients requires manual typing or tedious maintenance, users will abandon the app [P02:t8].
- Rigid weekly planning versus daily reality: The idea brief assumes a weekly dinner plan is the preferred format [idea_brief]. Evidence shows weekly plans frequently collapse due to schedule shifts, fatigue, or missing ingredients, causing guilt and abandonment [P03:t4, P05:t1, P05:t4]. Daily-decision users prefer same-day improvisation over predetermined weekly schedules [P05:t1, P08:t1].
- Fit restricted to weekly batch shoppers: A weekly plan paired with a weekly shopping list only accommodates households that shop once per week [idea_brief, P01:t5, P03:t5, P04:t5]. It fails to serve households that buy groceries daily or improvise as they go [P05:t5, P09:t5].
- Weak support for the "stop arguing" value proposition: The idea brief claims PantryPal will stop households arguing about dinner [idea_brief]. Only one interview turn describes arguing about dinner decisions [P02:t3]. The real friction is shopping coordination and duplicate buying, not relationship conflict [P02:t2, P04:t4, P10:t2].
- Low willingness to pay and conversion risk: Median market willingness to pay is EUR 3.50/month [S05]. Users demand explicit proof of grocery savings before paying, and several demand free tiers [P01:t9, P03:t9, P04:t9, P05:t9, S05].

## 7. What to test next
Recommended sequential experiments:
- Experiment 1: Low-effort inventory onboarding test (Tests the riskiest assumption of inventory logging effort before building the planner) [P02:t8, idea_brief].
  - Method: Prototype test comparing receipt scanning, barcode scanning, and quick-tap ingredient lists against manual typing with 20 target households [P02:t8].
  - Success metric: Greater than 70% of participants complete initial pantry setup in under 3 minutes, and at least 60% maintain inventory updates for 7 consecutive days [P02:t8].
- Experiment 2: Weekly plan vs. same-day dinner recommendation test (Tests planner format and open questions on user flexibility) [P05:t1, P08:t1, idea_brief].
  - Method: Two-week concierge test with 30 cooking households: Group A receives a Sunday weekly dinner plan; Group B receives daily 4:00 PM "cook tonight from your fridge" recommendations [P05:t1, P05:t2, P08:t1].
  - Success metric: Plan adherence rate and reported reduction in decision fatigue higher in Group B than Group A (target >65% meal completion) [P05:t1, P05:t4].
- Experiment 3: Shared shopping list sync smoke test (Tests coordination and duplicate prevention value proposition) [P02:t2, P04:t4].
  - Method: Lightweight shared list tool deployed with 15 multi-shopper households to evaluate if duplicate purchases are eliminated [P02:t2, P04:t4].
  - Success metric: Zero duplicate purchases reported across test households over two weeks [P04:t4].
- Experiment 4: Landing page pricing and value proposition test (Tests market demand and willingness to pay) [idea_brief, S05].
  - Method: Painted-door landing page testing messaging ("Save EUR 30/week on food waste" vs. "Stop deciding what's for dinner") at EUR 3.49/month and EUR 4.49/month price points [C:PantryGenie, C:SupperSync, S05].
  - Success metric: Email signup and call-to-action click-through rate exceeding 5% at EUR 3.49/month [S05].

## 8. Unsupported or weak claims
Claims from the brief and research materials that lack valid empirical support:
- Claim: "Existing recipe apps give ideas but do not know what you already own." [idea_brief]. Status: Contradicted by competitor records showing FridgeChef generates recipes from fridge photos and PantryGenie tracks pantry inventory with expiry alerts [C:FridgeChef, C:PantryGenie].
- Claim: PantryPal stops households arguing about dinner [idea_brief]. Status: Weakly supported; mentioned by only one couple in a single turn, whereas coordination breakdowns and duplicate purchases are the actual evidenced frictions [P02:t2, P02:t3, P04:t4].
- Claim: Households are willing to pay EUR 30 per month for meal planning [P08:t9]. Status: Weak and unrepresentative outlier based on a single two-hundred-euro holiday spoilage incident, contradicted by typical market stated WTP of EUR 3.50/month and competitor price ceilings of EUR 6.99/month [C:MealLoop, P08:t2, P08:t9, S05].
- Claim: The meal-planning and meal-kit market is growing at 25% annually [S06]. Status: Unsupported; source lacks year, methodology, and provenance [S06].
- Claim: 73% of families struggle with dinner decisions [S07]. Status: Unsupported; citation provides no source organization, methodology, or publication date [S07].
- Claim: 97% of households would pay for automated meal planning [S08]. Status: Unreliable; sourced from an informal blog post and heavily contradicted by actual market penetration (11%) and consumer stated preferences [P03:t9, P05:t9, S03, S08].
- Claim: Only 4% of grocery purchases are made online [S09]. Status: Stale; data originates from 2016 and does not reflect current grocery purchasing habits [P01:t5, S09].
- Claim: Time spent deciding what to cook is 22 minutes per day [S04]. Status: Usable with caveat; reflects 2023 data rather than current-year metrics [S04].
- Data validity note on WeekWise: WeekWise product details lack a source URL and cannot be independently verified [C:WeekWise].
