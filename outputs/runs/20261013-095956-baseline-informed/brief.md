# PantryPal discovery brief

## 1. Recommendation
The recommendation is to test further before committing to build the full product [idea_brief]. While households report real friction regarding food waste and the daily chore of deciding what to cook, the central premise that users will maintain an up-to-date pantry inventory is unproven and poses severe retention risk [P01:t10, P02:t8]. Furthermore, willingness to pay is fragile and tightly conditioned on tangible grocery savings, requiring quick low-fidelity tests on inventory logging and dinner decision formats before software development [P01:t9, P02:t9, S05].

## 2. Who the users are
The idea brief defines the target customer as households of two or more people who cook at home on most weeknights [idea_brief]. However, qualifying as target customer does not mean the household experiences the problem or wants an app [P09:t1, P09:t2].

Across the 5 usable in-target interviews, distinct user segments emerge:
- Working parents / families with children (2 of 2 usable interviews: P01, P04; note that P03 is a single-parent household and P06 is excluded): Characterised by time scarcity, weeknight time limits of thirty minutes, and managing competing children preferences [P01:t1, P01:t8, P04:t1, P04:t8]. Single parent P03 also fits this segment profile but operates on a constrained budget [P03:t1, P03:t3].
- Busy dual-income couples without children (1 of 2 usable in-target interviews: P02; P09 represents retired couples): Arrive home late, suffer from uncoordinated shopping, and drift into takeout when tired [P02:t1, P02:t2, P02:t3].
- Established routine couples (1 of 2 in-target couples: P09, sample size 2 in-target couples): Cook every day but have decades-long habits, reporting that dinner decisions are not a problem and rejecting apps [P09:t1, P09:t2].
- Adjacent segments (3 partial-fit interviews: P05, P08, P10): Freelancers with irregular routines who reject rigid plans (P05), frequent work travellers experiencing sporadic fridge spoilage (P08), and shared flatmates cooking independently while battling shared grocery miscoordination (P10) [P05:t1, P08:t2, P08:t3, P10:t1, P10:t2].
- Out-of-target contrast (1 interview: P07): Single student living alone who cooks once a week, views dinner planning apps as irrelevant, and expects free software [P07:t1, P07:t2, P07:t9].

Shopping rhythm serves as a key behavioural differentiator: families and budget planners shop weekly with occasional top-ups (2 of 3 in-target family interviews: P03, P04; and P01 orders weekly), fitting weekly meal schedules, whereas daily shoppers (P05, P09) resist forward planning [P01:t5, P03:t5, P04:t5, P05:t5, P09:t5].

## 3. Problems that are real
- Perishable food waste and forgetting ingredients: Real and evidenced by past behaviour. In-target participants report specific instances where bought items spoiled before use, such as spinach going slimy or duplicate basil purchases rotting (raw support: 4 of 5 in-target households [P01:t4, P02:t2, P02:t4, P03:t2]; weighted support: 3.30 of 4.20 in-target weight; adjacent support: P05:t2, P08:t2, P10:t2) [P01:t4, P02:t2, P02:t4, P03:t2, P05:t2, P08:t2, P10:t2].
- Daily mental load of deciding dinner: Evidenced by past behaviours where fatigue led to fallback meals or unplanned takeout (raw support: 3 of 5 in-target households [P01:t2, P01:t3, P02:t3, P04:t2]; weighted support: 2.60 of 4.20 in-target weight; adjacent support: P05:t2, P05:t3) [P01:t2, P01:t3, P02:t3, P04:t2, P05:t2, P05:t3]. However, answers specifically addressing frustration were directly prompted by interview questions [P01:t2, P02:t2, P03:t2, P04:t2].
- Shopping list and purchase miscoordination: Real behavioural friction in multi-person households where partners buy duplicates or miss needed items, ending up with duplicate chicken packs or empty pantries (raw support: 2 of 5 in-target households [P02:t2, P02:t4, P04:t2, P04:t4]; weighted support: 1.60 of 4.20 in-target weight; adjacent support: 1 of 1 flatmate household [P10:t2]) [P02:t2, P02:t4, P04:t2, P04:t4, P10:t2].

## 4. What the evidence says
- Behavioural evidence: Households routinely default to basic low-effort tools. Current tools include notes apps, paper pads on the fridge, and shared basic list apps (raw support: 4 of 5 in-target households [P01:t6, P02:t6, P03:t6, P04:t6]; weighted support: 3.30 of 4.20; adjacent support: P10:t6) [P01:t6, P02:t6, P03:t6, P04:t6, P10:t6]. Past attempts at structured meal-planning apps failed due to rigidity, with users abandoning them when plans lapsed [P05:t4].
- Stated requirements: Participants explicitly state that setup friction is fatal, stating "Setup has to be fast." and "If I need to type in my whole pantry I'll quit on day two." [P02:t8, P05:t8, P07:t8, P08:t8, P10:t8]. They demand that meals take "Thirty minutes at most." and reject rigid automation: "I want to edit the plan easily. I don't want an app dictating to me." [P01:t8, P02:t8, P03:t8, P04:t8, P05:t8]. Note that identical templated phrasing across interviews indicates upper-bound thematic clustering rather than fully independent formulation.
- Contradictions in data: In interview P03, the participant states "Planning is my favourite part of the week, honestly. I would never want an app to take that away from me." [P03:t1], yet in the next turn claims "Deciding is exhausting. I would rather cook anything than think about what to cook." [P03:t2]. In P04, the transcript shows attribution inconsistency where the persona mentions "my wife" and later "my husband" [P04:t2, P04:t7]. In P09, the participant reports decades of fixed shopping routines at one stall [P09:t1, P09:t3] alongside shopping almost every day without coordination [P09:t5, P09:t7]. Confidence on these points is correspondingly reduced.
- Willingness to pay: WTP is stated intent, not validated behaviour. When asked, in-target participants state a willingness of a few euros a month (P01, P04) or five to six euros (P02), provided it clearly cuts grocery spending [P01:t9, P02:t9, P04:t9]. Other respondents demand a free or ad-supported model [P03:t9, P05:t9, P09:t9]. In adjacent segment P08, an outlier offer of thirty euros a month was stated [P08:t9], but this was anchored on an extreme one-off holiday spoilage event rather than regular dinner planning [P08:t2, P08:t4], and is excluded from typical willingness to pay.

## 5. Competitors and market
- Existing competitors: The idea brief claims existing apps do not know what you already own [idea_brief], but this is contradicted by market solutions. FridgeChef offers recipe suggestions from photos of fridge contents at EUR 4.99/month (verified 2026-08-12) [C:FridgeChef]. PantryGenie tracks pantry inventory with barcode scanning and expiry alerts at EUR 3.99/month (verified 2026-06-18) [C:PantryGenie]. MealLoop targets families with weekly meal plans and automated grocery lists at EUR 6.99/month or EUR 49.99/year (verified 2026-07-30) [C:MealLoop]. SupperSync manages shared household dinner plans and lists at EUR 4.49/month (verified 2026-08-25) [C:SupperSync]. PlanPlate offers simple shared calendars at EUR 2.99/month, though data is older (verified 2024-11-05) [C:PlanPlate]. ZeroWaste Kitchen provides community use-it-up leftover recipes for free, though its record is also older (verified 2025-01-20) [C:ZeroWaste Kitchen]. WeekWise lists budget meal plans for EUR 29.99 one-off (verified 2026-05-02), but its details are unsourced due to missing source URL [C:WeekWise].
- Differentiation: True differentiation does not lie in inventory tracking alone or weekly meal planning alone, but in combining inventory awareness with dynamic weekly planning to minimise grocery waste [C:FridgeChef, C:MealLoop, C:PantryGenie].
- Market evidence: European households discard 19% of the food they buy [S01]. The average weekly grocery spend for a two-person household is EUR 118 [S02]. Only 11% of households currently use a meal-planning app [S03]. Adults spend 22 minutes each day deciding what to cook, though this finding dates from 2023 [S04]. The median stated willingness to pay for a cooking app is EUR 3.50 per month [S05]. Competitor benchmarks and market data set the realistic pricing ceiling between EUR 2.99 and EUR 6.99 per month [C:PlanPlate, C:MealLoop, S05].

## 6. Risks and open assumptions
- Inventory upkeep risk (highest risk): Households may refuse to manually record initial inventory or log consumed ingredients, causing recommendations to degrade rapidly [P01:t10, P02:t8, P08:t8].
- Format mismatch risk: A fixed weekly dinner plan may feel too rigid for households whose evenings fluctuate, creating guilt and abandonment [P05:t1, P05:t4]. Whether households prefer weekly batches versus same-day suggestions is an open assumption [P05:t1, P08:t1].
- Target customer validity: The assumption that all multi-person weeknight-cooking households need or want a meal planner is false, as demonstrated by routine-bound couples [P09:t1, P09:t2].
- Value proposition reframing: The claim that PantryPal will stop arguments about what is for dinner lacks robust evidence and is better framed as solving multi-buyer shopping coordination [idea_brief, P02:t2, P02:t3, P04:t2].
- Economic viability risk: Given a median app willingness to pay of EUR 3.50 per month, subscription pricing above EUR 5 to EUR 6 per month is completely untested and risks high churn [P02:t9, S05].

## 7. What to test next
1. Low-friction inventory capture test: Test whether target households will maintain a minimal inventory via receipt scan or quick-tap checklists versus abandoning manual input. Propose recruiting 40 working parents and dual-income couples to log fridge contents for 7 days; success metric is 60% maintaining inventory accuracy after 5 days [UNSUPPORTED].
2. Weekly planner versus same-day suggestion test: Run a concierge test comparing a structured 5-day dinner plan against a daily 4:00 PM suggestion based on available ingredients. Propose 30 target households split across both formats; success metric is 50% higher meal completion rate and lower abandonment on same-day suggestions [UNSUPPORTED].
3. Value proposition and pricing landing page smoke test: Test ad copy emphasising reduced grocery waste and shopping sync at EUR 3.99/month and EUR 4.99/month price points. Propose running 1,000 targeted ad clicks per variant; success metric is a minimum 5% email sign-up conversion rate [UNSUPPORTED].
4. Household shopping coordination test: Provide paired partners with a synchronised shared ingredient list to measure reduction in duplicate purchases. Propose testing with 20 couples over two weekly shopping cycles; success metric is zero duplicate purchases reported [UNSUPPORTED].

## 8. Unsupported or weak claims
- Research method exclusion: Interview P06 is completely excluded from customer evidence due to severe interviewer bias where almost every question was leading, and answers duplicated P01 word-for-word [P06:t1, P06:t2, P06:t8, P06:t9].
- Unsubstantiated market claims: The claim of 25% annual market growth in meal planning lacks a dated source [S06]. The claim that 73% of families struggle with dinner decisions is unsourced [S07]. The claim that 97% of households would pay for automated planning comes from an unreliable blog source and is rejected [S08]. The stat that 4% of grocery purchases are made online is stale (from 2016) and cannot represent current online grocery habits [S09].
- Stop arguing claim: The idea brief's claim that the product will stop households arguing about dinner is supported by only one couple mentioning a ten-minute argument (P02:t3) and is weakly supported as a primary marketing pillar [idea_brief, P02:t3].
- Outlier pricing: Stated willingness to pay of EUR 30 per month from P08 is an unvalidated outlier anchored to a post-vacation fridge incident, not weekly routine planning [P08:t2, P08:t9].
